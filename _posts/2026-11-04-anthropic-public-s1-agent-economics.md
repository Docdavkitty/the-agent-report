## TL;DR

Anthropic publicly filed its S-1 on October 26. Its filing reports $16.286 billion in revenue and a $693 million operating loss for the first half of 2026. A much larger net loss includes a significant fair-value charge, so it must not be treated as a cash-burn figure. The filing does not separately establish how much revenue comes from agents.

## A filing, not a completed IPO

The SEC's EDGAR index records Anthropic's public Form S-1 filing on **October 26, 2026**. Anthropic says it intends to list on Nasdaq under the symbol **ANT**, but its announcement says the share count and proposed price range have not yet been determined. Filing a registration statement is not the same as completing a public offering. *(Source : [SEC — Anthropic S-1 filing index](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/0001628280-26-067168-index.htm); [Anthropic — Proposed IPO announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/s1-sec))*

For an agent-focused publication, the prospectus is a useful reality check. It shows the scale and costs of an AI provider whose products include models used by agents. It does not, by itself, turn every model dollar into identifiable agent revenue.

## Revenue grew sharply, but the period matters

The S-1's consolidated statement reports revenue of **$4.585 billion for all of 2025** and **$16.286 billion for the six months ended June 30, 2026**. The comparable six-month figure for 2025 was **$1.163 billion**. These figures are consolidated company revenue, not a separately disclosed agent segment. The interim 2026 figures are unaudited, and a half-year is not a forecast for the full year. *(Source : [SEC — Anthropic S-1, consolidated statements](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm))*

The temptation is to multiply the first-half number by two and treat it as a forecast. That assumes a stable revenue trajectory, which the filing does not promise. Model pricing, usage patterns, large customer contracts and competition can all change. Comparing the half-year to the previous full year is a useful scale check, but it is not a like-for-like growth rate.

The first-half comparison is more meaningful because the periods have the same length. Even then, it describes a historical change, not a guaranteed trajectory. A buyer assessing an agent vendor should still distinguish contracted access, actual use, model consumption and a completed business task.

## Operating loss versus net loss

The filing reports a **$693 million operating loss** for the first half of 2026 and an **$80.748 billion net loss**. Those numbers measure different things. The same statement includes a **$72.388 billion loss from the change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and SAFEs**. *(Source : [SEC — Anthropic S-1, consolidated statements](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm))*

That distinction is essential. Net loss is an accounting result; it is not a synonym for cash spent during the period. The large fair-value adjustment is part of the reported result, but readers should consult the cash-flow statement and the underlying notes before making a claim about cash consumption. Nor should the smaller operating loss be confused with positive cash flow or profitability.

The prospectus reports **$8.945 billion in compute and infrastructure expense** for the first half of 2026. This is a company-level expense line, not a marginal cost per agent action. It underlines why infrastructure costs matter, but it does not reveal the cost of any individual Claude Code session, customer workflow or successful agent task. *(Source : [SEC — Anthropic S-1, consolidated statements](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm))*

## What is missing from an agent-level analysis

To calculate the share of revenue attributable specifically to agents, readers would need a clearly defined segment and a corresponding revenue breakdown. A model API call can power a conversational application, a coding assistant, a retrieval workflow or a more autonomous agent. Without an appropriate disclosed breakdown, assigning the consolidated number to any one of those categories would be speculation.

A stronger operational analysis would separate revenue by product and customer use, then compare it with inference, training, support and distribution costs. Even that would not settle the buyer's question: the value of an agent depends on completed work, supervision and errors, not merely provider revenue.

The filing also should not be used to validate a reported future valuation or an IPO date absent a specific disclosure. The company says the proposed offering's price range and share count remain undetermined. *(Source : [Anthropic — S-1 announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/s1-sec))*

## Why this matters for builders

A provider's financial statement offers context on scale and risk, but it is not a substitute for a procurement review. Builders should assess model availability, pricing terms, data handling and fallback strategies separately. Customers should monitor their own task-level costs and quality: a provider's aggregate economics cannot tell them whether a particular workflow saves money.

The most revealing takeaway is therefore also a restraint: the first public filing makes the provider's financial picture much clearer while leaving the specific economics of agent products largely unresolved in the figures examined here.

## FAQ

### Has Anthropic already completed its IPO?

The sources establish a proposed offering and a public registration filing, not a completed IPO.

### Is the $80.748 billion net loss equal to cash burn?

No. It includes substantial fair-value adjustments; cash flows require separate analysis.

### How much of revenue comes from agents?

The consolidated figures cited here do not provide an agent-specific revenue share.
